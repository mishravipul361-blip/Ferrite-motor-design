How the copper cross-section is chosen

  • Uniform: each track is one width all the way round.
  • Each section at the J limit: every track segment, spoke body and the swap piece is cut to I ÷ (J × t), never narrower than the sheet thickness.
  • Least copper: every section is first cut to the current-density limit; then the fewest grams are added back, widening the segments that lower the imbalance most per gram, until the imbalance limit is met.
  • Sized for balance: each track segment is set narrow (J limit), normal, or widened into the free band around the lead circle between other-net weld pads, whichever gives the lowest worst-phase imbalance. Copper weight is ignored.
  • Path balancing (separate switch): extra series resistance in the stronger path of each phase; brings the imbalance to 0 % but is an extra part to build.

Why the imbalance cannot reach zero with copper alone: each phase has two parallel paths that join the open rings about half a ring apart, while the three terminals must sit close together for one cable exit. The path whose welds are far from the terminals sees more ring resistance, and the ring segments also carry the neighbouring phase's current. More terminal span or wider copper on the weaker path reduce it; only extra resistance removes it.

How the goals are scored

Each goal is a weighted sum of six terms, each divided by its v7 value or its rule limit (so 1.0 = as v7 / at the limit): busbar loss, height, copper, path imbalance, terminal span, cable exit. The named goal puts weight 4 on its own term and 0.3 or less on the others, so “Least copper” is mostly copper but still breaks ties on loss and imbalance. Every design must first pass every busbar rule. Search: (1) every terminal arrangement on a reference geometry, (2) a Latin-hypercube sample of all geometry inputs, uniform and J-limit sections, (3) a pattern search per goal, (4) the 10 lowest-imbalance geometries sized for balance, (5) the 10 lightest J-limit designs widened to meet the imbalance limit, (6) a final polish of each goal's winner: every section mode, then ±1 grid step on every input until nothing improves.

Both imbalances can be read as an extra current that flows round a loop on top of the useful current. Values below are at the line current used throughout, 360 A rms (phase current 207.8 A rms in the delta); “peak” is the instantaneous peak (× √2). 1 · Path imbalance → current circulating between the two parallel paths of a phase

With two paths per phase, I₁ = I(1 + p/2) and I₂ = I(1 − p/2), each I = Iphase/2. The part that only goes round the loop formed by the two paths is (I₁ − I₂)/2 = p · Iphase/4. The hotter path's two coils carry p/2 more current and so dissipate about p more copper loss; the whole winding's extra loss is only (p/2)².

How much is acceptable?

  • The cost of path imbalance is heat in the hotter path's two coils: about p % more copper loss there, so roughly p % more copper-loss temperature rise in those coils (e.g. 4 % → +4 %, i.e. +3.2 K on an 80 K copper-loss rise). Below about 2 % this is lost in the tolerance of the wire and the welds; 2–5 % is acceptable if the hot-spot margin covers +p %; above 5 % it starts to eat a real part of the thermal margin. This is an engineering guide, not a standard: use your own hot-spot margin.
  • The current circulating round the delta is about 1 A here (under 0.5 % of the phase current) and its loss is below 0.01 %: line-to-line imbalance at these levels is a measurement/tolerance matter, not a thermal one. The 1.5 % rule on the page is the usual end-of-line acceptance check for winding resistance.
  • These are DC (stall / low-speed) values, the worst case: at speed the coil inductance dominates the path impedance and the split evens out. Coils of identical geometry give no EMF-driven circulating current; unequal back-EMF (eccentricity, magnet tolerance) would add a separate circulating current that this resistive model does not include.

How these results were checked

How the checking was done

Four independent checks ran in parallel on the exported results, each by a separate agent that did not share the page's code: (1) a mathematical re-solve of every design's circuit in Python/numpy, (2) a cross-case consistency check of every number and rule, with a written reason why each winner is the optimum, (3) a local-optimality test that nudged every input of every winner one and two grid steps in the real page, and (4) a visual check of all 38 pictures against the data. A fifth agent reviewed the page's code for bugs. Every bug they confirmed was fixed, the cases were re-run, and the checks were repeated on the final results.

1 · Mathematics (independent re-solve)

An agent wrote its own nodal solver in Python/numpy and re-solved every design's exported circuit (the exact branches and resistances the page used), with all three phases at 360 A. Result: 0 discrepancies in all 12 designs, largest differences ≈ 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹¹. It reproduced every path current and path imbalance per phase, all three line-to-line resistances, every loss, each part's current and current density (J = I/A), and J max. Kirchhoff's current law holds at every node, the two coils of each series pair carry identical current, and the numbers stored by the optimiser equal the numbers measured after loading each design.

2 · Consistency and why each design is optimal

A second agent checked every design against every rule and against the other designs. All 12 designs are feasible (the only failing rules are the two coil-bend rules, which belong to the coil CAD). Each goal's winner has the best score for its goal among all designs that satisfy that case's rules, and every relaxation moves the right way: terminals within 60° and 90° lower the imbalance, a 15 A/mm² limit and a 6 % imbalance limit lower the copper. Masses, losses, imbalances and J are internally consistent (every part loss = I²R, every mass = length × width × thickness × density). Its written reasons are in each design's “Why this is the optimum” box.

3 · Local optimality (every input nudged ±1 and ±2 grid steps in the page)

A third agent took each winner into the real page and tried 44–48 neighbours: every input ±1 and ±2 grid steps, every terminal order, every ring arrangement and every cross-section mode. In the final run 8 of 11 winners are strict local optima; the other three (balanced, least copper, J ≤ 15) can be improved by at most 0.05 % by a half-step move. The binding constraints are the same everywhere: any thicker or wider copper hits the in-plane gap and the radial limits, and the terminals are boxed by the lug pitch, the span limit and “terminal on its own ring”. Earlier rounds of this test found that the optimiser was not fully converged (up to 1.7 % left on the table) and that the 60°/90° cases could land in a worse region; both were fixed (final polish stage; earlier winners fed back as seeds).

4 · Pictures checked against the data

A fourth agent looked at all 38 pictures and compared them with the data: the cross-section style claimed (uniform / at the J limit / widened), the largest current-density label against J max and the limit, sampled area labels against each part's area (widened parts show “normal – widened” and the widened value equals t × widened width), and the terminal positions against the span. All checks pass. It found and we fixed: a wrong “widened” label on the 6 % design (nothing was widened there), area labels that hid the widening, and balancing labels overlapping terminal labels (now on the plan only; 3D shows red markers).

Bugs found during this check and fixed

  • Optimiser ignored your operating conditions (line current, temperature, joint resistances): every candidate was solved at the defaults, and loading a result overwrote your settings. Now it always uses the settings on screen.
  • Least-copper sizing leaked between designs: loading one design after another kept the previous design's sizing density (a design shown as 148 g came out as 180 g). Fixed; the “Optimised default” button also resets the section mode.
  • Custom weights: the goal table kept the scores from the run; it now recomputes every goal score when weights change.
  • Feasibility verdicts disagreed between the top strip, Checks, Results and Optimize tabs (different rule sets). All now use one verdict: every busbar rule plus the Geometry-tab limits.
  • Weld pads under-modelled when spokes are narrowed (4 mm instead of 5 mm), which overstated clearances by 0.5 mm; the weld-to-weld rule had the same 4 mm assumption. Both now use the real pad width.
  • Widened segments drawn with rounded ends in the clearance check, which reported false 2.3 mm gaps; they are now square steps, as stamped.
  • Optimiser not fully converged: winners could still be improved by 0.01–1.7 % by another section mode or a small step. A final polish stage now tries every section mode and then steps every input until nothing improves, and earlier winners are fed back in so a relaxed rule never gives a worse result.
  • Terminal gaps capped at 25°, so “terminals within” above 50° had no effect; terminal blade fixed at 10 × 3 mm, so no current-density limit below 12 A/mm² was feasible; joint resistance 0 µΩ gave NaN; the balancing switch turned itself on during re-runs; labels overlapped (balancing values, widened areas). All fixed.

Known limits of the model

  • DC/low-frequency resistive model: at speed the coil inductance dominates and evens out the path split, so the path imbalance shown is the stall (worst) case.
  • The two coil-bend rules (corner bends 26.8 %, layer-change step 35.1 % against ≤ 25 %) fail in every design: they are properties of the coil CAD, not the busbar.
  • Path balancing resistances are modelled as extra jumper wire; the routing room for that wire is not checked.
  • Widened segments are modelled as square steps; current crowding at the steps is not modelled (it slightly raises local J at the step corners).
  • The terminal blade (≥ 30 mm²) carries the full line current, so the current-density rule is set by the terminals (12.0 A/mm²) in every design.
